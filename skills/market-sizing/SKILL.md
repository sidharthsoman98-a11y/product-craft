# Market sizing

An estimate is judged on the structure and the sense-check, not on the number. The number
will be wrong; the reasoning is the deliverable.

## Procedure

1. **Clarify the question.** Units (people, transactions, rupees, devices), geography,
   time period, and whether it is annual flow or installed stock. Ask only if genuinely
   ambiguous, otherwise state the interpretation and continue.
2. **Choose the segmentation that does the work.** Population-based, household-based,
   supply-based (counting outlets, stores, vehicles, agents), or usage-occasion-based.
   Supply-based estimates are usually more defensible than population-based ones because
   the counting unit is observable, and choosing that path signals experience.
3. **Build bottom-up as the primary path.** Each step should be a quantity somebody could
   verify. Prefer a chain of four or five factors; longer chains multiply error.
4. **Build top-down as the cross-check**, from a known aggregate. If the two differ by more
   than a factor of three, find the disagreement rather than averaging. The disagreement is
   the most interesting part of the answer.
5. **Sense-check against an anchor** you actually know: a published aggregate, a comparable
   market, a per-capita ratio, or a physical constraint (hours in a day, shelf space,
   delivery capacity per rider hour). State the anchor.
6. **Segment the answer** into TAM, SAM and SOM, where SOM is bounded by a real constraint:
   distribution reach, capacity, capital, or realistic share in a defined time.
7. **Sensitivity.** Name the one or two assumptions carrying the estimate and give the range
   they imply. Present the answer as a range with a central case.
8. **So what.** State the decision the number supports. A size with no decision attached is
   trivia, and interviewers ask "so what" precisely to see whether the candidate knows this.

## Discipline

- Round aggressively during arithmetic; carry precision only at the end.
- Say the arithmetic out loud in the write-up so it can be checked.
- Never present a recalled statistic as fact. If one is used, mark it as recalled, give it
  a range, and show that the conclusion survives the low end.
- Adjust for the market: income distribution, smartphone and connectivity tiers, urban and
  rural split, informal-sector share, and seasonality. Applying a developed-market
  penetration rate to an emerging market is the single most common sizing error.

## Output

Structure, assumptions table with basis, bottom-up chain with arithmetic, top-down
cross-check, reconciliation of the gap, range with central case, sense-check anchor, and
the decision it supports.
